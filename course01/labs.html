AI-CLD-1001 Lab Guide

Working Smarter with Claude Chat

Configuring Your Claude Workspace for Knowledge Work

Lab 1

  • Navigate the four areas of the Claude Chat interface and configure profile preferences that align Claude with your role and communication norms
  • Manage Claude’s memory and chat search to preserve useful professional context and exclude sensitive or one-off conversations
  • Compare responses from Opus 4.7, Sonnet 4.6, and Haiku 4.5 on the same task to build intuition for model selection

1.1. Introduction

Estimated time: 25 minutes

In this lab, you will set up Claude Chat as a personalized environment for knowledge work rather than treating it as a generic chat window. You will move through the interface intentionally, configure the settings that shape how Claude interprets you, take direct control of what Claude remembers across sessions, and finish by running the same task across all three current models so you can feel — not just read about — the difference in speed, depth, and tone. The goal is to make Claude a reliable extension of your working context before you attempt any serious business task.

 Note

This lab and all labs in this course are designed for the web version of Claude Chat. In addition, it assumes you are using a paid tier account, which provides access to all three models and the full suite of capabilities. If you are using a free tier account, you can still complete the lab but you may not have access to all three models or certain features. If that’s the case, just skip the activities that aren’t available to you and focus on understanding the concepts. Furthermore, some labs include the use of external files. Confirm with your instructor where to find these files.

1.2. Lab Overview

In this lab, you will complete the following activities:

  1. Tour the four areas of the Claude Chat interface and identify the purpose of each

  2. View and edit your memory, and directly instruct Claude to remember several facts you want retained across future sessions

  3. Conduct one conversation in incognito mode, then use chat search to retrieve verbatim content from a prior standalone conversation

  4. Run the same prompt on Opus 4.7, Sonnet 4.6, and Haiku 4.5, and compare response depth, tone, and latency

1.3. Tour the Four Areas of the Claude Chat Interface

Open Claude Chat at claude.ai in your browser and sign in. Take a few minutes to locate each of the four areas and complete the small task associated with it.

  • Area 1 — Left Sidebar (Navigation). Identify New Chat, Recents, Projects, and Artifacts.

  • Area 2 — Bottom Left (Personal Settings & Configuration). Click your initials in the lower-left corner to open the personal settings menu. This is where Claude learns how you work — it contains memory, styles, connectors, and account settings. You will work directly with memory in the next activity.

  • Area 3 — Center Panel (Conversation). Locate the input bar at the bottom of the chat. Note the model selector below it, the "+" button for file attachments and tool toggles, and the ghost icon in the upper right that starts an incognito session.

  • Area 4 — Right Panel (Artifacts & Project Details). Note that this area is contextual — it only opens when you are working with an artifact or inside a Project. You will not see it during a normal chat. We will work with this area in the next module.

1.4. View and Edit Your Memory

Memory is Claude’s synthesized model of who you are — your role, professional context, and working preferences. It is separate from your chat history and is auto-injected at the start of every new standalone conversation.

1.4.1. Confirm that Claude currently remembers nothing about you

  1. Navigate to Settings > Capabilities

  2. In the Memory section make sure that "Search and reference chats" and "Generate memory from chat history" are both toggled "On" (the button turns blue).

  3. You should see an option to "View and manage memory" appear if you need to toggle "On" the above options.

    • Alternatively, Claude might not display "View and manage memory" if it doesn’t have any memories about you yet. Don’t worry, you will create some in a minute.

    • Remember: if you are new to Claude (i.e. this is a new account for you), the memory will be empty or sparse — that is expected.

1.4.2. Instruct Claude directly to remember context about you

  1. Start a new chat and enter a prompt that establishes standing context.

    • Here’s an example memory instruction you can adapt:

      Please remember that I work in [your industry] as a [your role], and that when I ask for analysis I want a bottom-line-up-front summary followed by supporting detail.

    • Claude will confirm what it has stored in memory. (Watch for this message in the chat.)

1.4.3. Return to Settings > Capabilities > "View and edit memory"

  1. Verify the new entry appears.

  2. Edit or remove anything that is incorrect, then reflect:

    What kinds of information do you NOT want Claude to remember, and how would you keep it out?

1.5. Use Incognito Mode and Chat Search

Some conversations should not feed Claude’s memory or appear in your history — sensitive HR matters, confidential strategy work, or one-off lookups. Claude provides incognito mode for exactly these cases.

  1. Locate the ghost icon in the upper right of the chat panel.

  2. Click it to start an incognito session.

  3. Have a brief conversation about something sensitive or one-off.

    • Here’s an example of an incognito-appropriate prompt:

      Help me draft talking points for a difficult conversation with a team member about declining performance.

    • Notice that the incognito chat does not appear in your Recents.

1.5.1. Close the incognito session

1.5.2. Test chat search functionality

  1. Return to a new standalone (non-incognito) chat.

  2. Ask Claude to retrieve content from the memory chat you ran earlier in this lab.

    • Here’s an example chat search prompt:

      What did I ask you to remember earlier today?

  3. Watch the chat for a visible tool call indicating Claude is searching your chat history.

  4. The result should include a citation linking back to the source conversation.

1.5.3. Try asking for verbatim content from the incognito conversation

  • Example prompt:

    What were the talking points I drafted about the team member who exhibited declining performance?

  • Reflect:

    1. Did Claude retrieve verbatim content or a summary of what I told it to remember?

    2. Could Claude find content from my incognito conversation? Why or why not?

1.6. Run the Same Prompt on Three Models

  1. Now you will run the same prompt on each of the three current Claude models and compare the responses side by side.

  2. Use this prompt, or substitute one from your own work:

    In three bullets, name the biggest trade-off a consulting firm faces when deciding whether to train all employees on AI vs only client-facing consultants.

1.6.1. Run the prompt three separate times — each in a new chat

 Important

Do not switch models mid-conversation, as that may clear cached context and consume additional usage limits.

  1. Set the model to Claude Haiku 4.5. Submit the prompt. Note:

    1. How quickly the response started streaming

    2. Total response length (number of bullets, sentences, or words)

    3. Whether the trade-offs identified are surface-level or substantive

    4. Whether the response feels complete or feels rushed

  2. Start a new chat. Set the model to Claude Sonnet 4.6. Submit the same prompt. Compare to Haiku:

    1. Did response time noticeably increase?

    2. Did Sonnet identify different or deeper trade-offs than Haiku?

    3. Did Sonnet add nuance, qualifications, or second-order considerations Haiku missed?

    4. Does the tone feel more polished or executive-ready?

  3. Start a new chat. Set the model to Claude Opus 4.7. Submit the same prompt. Compare to both prior runs:

    1. How much longer did Opus take vs. Haiku and Sonnet?

    2. Did Opus surface trade-offs neither of the other models considered?

    3. Is the reasoning visibly more structured or rigorous?

    4. For this specific prompt, did the extra depth justify the extra latency?

1.6.2. Review your three outputs side by side and reflect

  1. Which model produced the most usable response for this specific task?

  2. Was the difference in depth proportional to the difference in latency?

  3. For your daily work, which model would you default to? When would you escalate to Opus 4.7? When would you drop to Haiku 4.5?

1.7. Challenge Activity (If Time Permits)

  1. Open one of the three model-comparison chats

  2. Switch the model mid-conversation by selecting a different model from the model selector below the input bar.

  3. Then send a follow-up prompt — for example, "Tighten your previous answer to three bullet points."

  4. Observe whether the response feels slower than usual, and notice if Claude acknowledges that the model has changed.

    • This demonstrates why a new chat is preferable when you genuinely need a different model.

1.8. Key Takeaways

Claude Chat is a configured environment for knowledge work, not a generic chatbox:

  • Memory gives Claude standing context about your role, work, and preferences across every new conversation

  • Memory and chat history are independent systems with independent controls in Settings > Capabilities

  • Incognito mode is the right choice for sensitive or one-off conversations — it bypasses both memory and chat history

  • Match the model to the task: Haiku 4.5 for speed, Sonnet 4.6 for daily work, Opus 4.7 when accuracy and depth matter most

Prompting, Grounding, and Tool Selection for Business Tasks

Lab 2

  • Apply the C-L-E-A-R framework to transform a vague prompt into a precise, actionable instruction
  • Ground Claude’s responses in an uploaded source document to reduce generic output and improve factual accuracy
  • Select and use the appropriate tool based on the business task — web search, adaptive thinking, or Research mode

2.1. Introduction

Estimated time: 40 minutes

In this lab, you will move from personalization into production — using Claude to generate real, defensible work product. You will start with a deliberately weak prompt and progressively sharpen it using the C-L-E-A-R framework, then add a source document so Claude works from your material rather than its general knowledge. You will then complete three short, targeted tasks — one using web search, one using adaptive thinking, and one using Research mode — so you leave the lab with first-hand experience of when each tool is the right choice.

2.2. Lab Overview

In this lab, you will complete the following activities:

  1. Rewrite a provided vague business prompt by applying the Concise, Logical, and Explicit principles

  2. Iterate on your rewritten prompt using the Adaptive and Reflective principles, revising based on Claude’s initial response

  3. Upload a source document and instruct Claude to answer using only that material

  4. Complete a Web Search task: use Claude to find and cite current, factual information on a specified business topic

  5. Complete an Adaptive Thinking task: use Claude to work through a multi-step analytical problem with no external sources

  6. Complete a Research Mode task: commission a multi-source comparative report and review the chain-of-thought log afterward

2.3. Rewrite the Vague Prompt with C-L-E

2.3.1. Start a new chat in Claude Chat

  1. Begin by submitting a deliberately weak prompt and observing the response.

    • Here’s an example of a vague business prompt:

      What should a SaaS company do when they expand to Europe? Tell me everything I need to know.

2.3.2. Submit the prompt and review the output

  • The response will likely be long, generic, and unfocused.

  • The prompt fails on three counts:

    • Concise: contains conversational filler ("when they", "Tell me everything I need to know")

    • Logical: mixes two different concepts — actions ("what to do") and information ("what to know")

    • Explicit: specifies no industry detail, no target countries, no audience, no format

2.3.3. Now rewrite the prompt by applying Concise, Logical, and Explicit

  1. Pick one concept (actions OR information), strip the filler, and add the missing specifics.

    • Here’s an example of an improved prompt using C-L-E:

      You are advising a U.S.-based HR compliance SaaS company entering the UK, Germany, and France. Identify the top three strategic decisions they must make in their first year of expansion.

2.3.4. Run the improved prompt in the same chat

  1. Compare the two responses.

  2. Reflect:

    1. Did the improved prompt produce a more focused, business-ready response?

    2. Which of the three principles — Concise, Logical, or Explicit — had the biggest impact?

    3. What is still missing from the response?

2.4. Iterate the Prompt with A-R

The Concise, Logical, and Explicit principles get you a usable first draft. Adaptive and Reflective drive what happens next: review Claude’s response, identify what is missing or off-target, and revise the prompt accordingly.

  1. Review the response Claude gave to your improved C-L-E prompt

  2. Identify at least one of the following:

    1. A constraint you forgot to specify (length, format, audience, tone)

    2. An aspect of the response that is too generic, off-target, or hedge-laden

    3. A piece of structure that would make the output more decision-ready

  3. Now revise the prompt to address what you found

    • Here’s an example of a revised prompt using A and R:

      You are advising a U.S.-based HR compliance SaaS company entering the UK, Germany, and France over three years. Identify the top three strategic decisions they must make in their first year, ranked by financial risk. Format as an executive memo, 250 words maximum. Lead each decision with the recommendation, then a one-sentence rationale. Audience: a CFO with M&A background. Avoid hedging language.

2.4.1. Run the revised prompt

  1. Compare the new output to your C-L-E version.

  2. Reflect:

    1. What did adding ranking, format, and audience constraints unlock in the response?

    2. Did Claude follow your "avoid hedging" instruction, or did it slip back into generalities?

    3. Would another iteration improve it further? What would you change?

2.5. Ground Claude in a Source Document

Now you will add a real source document and explicitly restrict Claude to using only that material. This is the External Knowledge Restriction technique — it dramatically reduces hallucinations and forces Claude to ground its answers in your content rather than its general training data.

2.5.1. Start a new chat

  1. Upload market_entry_strategy.txt using the "+" button or by dragging the file into the chat input.

    • This file is located in the lab files directory; confirm with your instructor where to find it.

    • It contains a sample market entry strategy for a fictional SaaS company called CompliSure, which is expanding into Europe.

  2. Confirm the file is attached

2.5.2. Submit a prompt that explicitly tells Claude to use only the document

Here’s an example of a knowledge-restricted prompt:

Using ONLY the content of the uploaded document, answer the following: For each of the three target countries (UK, Germany, France), list the entry mode CompliSure plans to use and explain why that mode was chosen. If any information is not in the document, say so explicitly — do not infer or supplement from your general knowledge.

2.5.3. Review Claude’s response

  1. Verify each claim in the model’s response against the source document to ensure Claude only uses information from the document.

  2. Then ask a follow-up question that the document does NOT actually answer.

    • Here’s an example follow-up:

      Using only the document, what is CompliSure’s current market share in the UK and who are their top three named competitors?

  3. Review the response to see if Claude correctly identifies that this information is not in the document, or if it hallucinates an answer based on its training data.

    • Claude should acknowledge the information is not in the provided document, rather than fabricating an answer.

    • If Claude provides a specific answer without flagging uncertainty, revisit your prompt and try asking more directly.

  4. Reflect:

    1. Did Claude correctly identify that the market share and named competitors are not in the document?

    2. How does forcing Claude to use only one source change the tone and confidence of the response?

    3. When in your real work would the "use ONLY this material" instruction be valuable?

2.6. Complete a Web Search Task

Web search is the right tool when you need current, factual information that may have changed since Claude’s training data — recent regulations, market data, company announcements, or news.

2.6.1. Start a new chat

2.6.2. Enable web search via the "+" button (toggle Web search on)

2.6.3. Submit a prompt that requires current information and citations

Here’s an example web search prompt:

Search the web for the most recent EU GDPR enforcement actions against SaaS companies in 2025 and 2026. Provide a brief summary of two notable cases, the fine amounts, and the underlying violations. Include source citations and links.

2.6.4. Watch the visible search activity in the chat

2.6.5. Review the response and reflect

  1. Were the citations clickable and to credible sources?

  2. Did Claude cross-reference facts across multiple sources, or rely on a single one?

  3. When would web search be the wrong tool for the job?

2.7. Complete an Adaptive Thinking Task

Adaptive thinking is the right tool for complex multi-step reasoning where no external sources are needed — math, logic, financial modeling, weighing alternatives, ethics, or any task where careful step-by-step thought matters more than current data.

2.7.1. Start a new chat using Sonnet 4.6 or Opus 4.7

  • Both have adaptive thinking enabled by default.

  • You do not need to toggle anything — Claude will engage thinking automatically when the question warrants it.

2.7.2. Submit a prompt that requires multi-step thinking

Here’s an example adaptive thinking prompt:

CompliSure plans a 3-year European expansion with a $20M total investment budget. Year 1 consumes 40% of the budget with no revenue. Year 2 reaches break-even on operating costs. Year 3 must deliver a 25% return on the full $20M investment. Calculate (a) the required Year 3 revenue, (b) the average monthly Year 3 revenue, and (c) what the Year 3 revenue requirement implies about the average annual revenue per customer if CompliSure aims for 1,000 paying European customers by end of Year 3. Show your reasoning step by step.

 Tip

Watch for the "Thinking…​" indicator while Claude works through the math.

2.7.3. After the response arrives

  1. Expand the thinking section above the answer to see Claude’s exposed reasoning.

  2. Reflect:

    1. Did Claude’s exposed reasoning match the final numerical answer?

    2. Where did Claude pause and verify? Where did it commit quickly?

    3. If you had asked the same question with thinking off, would you trust the result?

Correct answers to the example prompt:

  • Year 3 revenue required = $20M * 1.25 = $25M

  • Average monthly Year 3 revenue = $25M / 12 ≈ $2.08M

  • Average annual revenue per customer = $25M / 1,000 = $25,000

2.8. Complete a Research Mode Task

Research mode runs multiple agentic searches that build on each other, producing a citation-backed comparative report. Use it when you need synthesis across many sources, not a single answer. Research mode can run from 5 to 30 minutes depending on scope, so you must constrain the scope clearly in your prompt or it will consume the rest of your lab time.

2.8.1. Start a new chat

  1. Enable Research mode via the "+" button

  2. The magnifying glass icon turns blue when on

2.8.2. Submit a tightly scoped research query with explicit limits

  • Here’s an example Research mode prompt with built-in scope constraints:

    Research the European HR compliance SaaS market. Identify three established vendors — ideally one each operating primarily in the UK, Germany, and France. For each vendor, provide: company name, target customer size, two key product differentiators, and one recent strategic move from the last 12 months. Limit research to no more than two sources per vendor. Keep total research time under 5 minutes.

  • While Research mode runs, observe the live progress display in the conversation panel. You may see some or all of the following, depending on how Claude approaches the task:

    • A running status indicator showing that Research is active, along with an elapsed time counter

    • A plan or task list Claude generates at the start, breaking your query into sub-questions it intends to investigate

    • A live stream of search queries Claude is sending to the web — these update as Claude decides what to look for next

    • Source URLs and page titles appearing as Claude retrieves them, often with a brief note on what Claude is extracting from each

    • Claude’s reasoning between searches — short notes explaining why a source was useful, why one was discarded, or why Claude is pivoting to a new line of investigation

    • A final synthesis step where Claude assembles the report and attaches citations to specific claims

2.8.3. After the report is delivered

  1. Expand the chain-of-thought log and review it.

  2. Reflect:

    1. Were the citations easy to verify? Did any look weak or low-quality?

    2. How does this output compare to what web search alone gave you in the earlier activity?

    3. When in your real work would Research mode be worth the extra time and usage cost?

2.9. Challenge Activity (If Time Permits)

  1. Take a real prompt from your own current work — something you have actually asked an AI tool, or would ask.

  2. Paste it into Claude and ask Claude to apply the C-L-E-A-R framework to revise it.

  3. Run both your original prompt and Claude’s revised version and compare the responses.

    • Note one specific improvement that came from the rewrite.

2.10. Key Takeaways

The C-L-E-A-R framework is iterative, not one-shot:

  • Concise, Logical, and Explicit get you a usable first draft

  • Adaptive and Reflective improve it through revision after seeing Claude’s response

Grounding and tool selection determine whether Claude’s output is defensible:

  • Use "use ONLY this material" instructions to ground Claude in your source documents and reduce hallucinations

  • Match the tool to the task: web search for current facts, adaptive thinking for complex reasoning, Research mode for multi-source synthesis

  • The right prompt and the right tool together produce business-ready output you can stand behind

Personalizing Claude with Preferences and Styles

Lab 3

  • Configure profile settings that establish consistent context across every Claude conversation
  • Apply built-in styles to adapt Claude’s tone, length, and format to different audiences and tasks
  • Create a custom style that reflects your own voice or a specific communication standard

3.1. Introduction

Estimated time: 25 minutes

In this lab, you will move beyond default Claude responses to shape both how Claude understands you and how it communicates back. You will configure profile settings that persist across every conversation, experiment with Claude’s built-in styles to see how the same prompt produces very different outputs, and create a custom style that captures your personal or professional voice. By the end, you will have a personalized Claude configuration ready to support your recurring work and audiences.

3.2. Lab Overview

In this lab, you will complete the following activities:

  1. Navigate to Settings and configure your profile to provide Claude with your name and instructions about your role, domain, typical tasks, and general communication preferences

  2. Run the same prompt before and after providing profile settings to observe the difference in Claude’s response

  3. Compare the Normal default style to three built-in styles (Concise, Formal, and Explanatory or Learning) to a single business prompt

  4. Compare the resulting outputs for tone, length, and structure, and identify which style fits which audience

  5. Create a custom style using one of three methods — uploading writing samples, describing the style in natural language, or using Custom Instructions (Advanced)

  6. Apply the custom style to a realistic writing task and evaluate whether it matches your intended voice

3.3. Configure Profile Settings and Observe the Difference

Profile settings are personal instructions Claude applies to every conversation. They tell Claude who you are and what you do — separate from styles, which control how Claude formats responses. Configure them once and Claude carries that context into every new chat.

3.3.1. Run a baseline prompt with no profile settings

 Note

This activity assumes you are using a "new" account and have not yet configured any profile settings. If you have existing settings, you can either clear them now or simply use what you currently have, submit the example prompt, and note the response as your baseline.

  1. Start a new chat and submit a casual, open-ended request that depends on knowing who you are.

    • Here’s an example baseline prompt:

      Help me prep for tomorrow’s leadership meeting.

  2. Submit and review.

    • Without defined profile settings, Claude has to ask follow-up questions or guess at your context.

3.3.2. Now configure your profile settings

  1. Click your initials in the lower-left corner

  2. Open Settings and choose General. Your profile settings are located at the top of the page.

  3. If your profile is blank, answer the questions presented to you and include instructions to Claude on how you like to receive information, what your role is, etc.

  4. Or edit the existing text if you already had profile settings

  5. In either case:

    1. Be more specific, and more detailed than you think is necessary — the goal is to give Claude rich context to draw from in every conversation

    2. This is the context Claude will use in every conversation to tailor responses to your needs and working style

      • Here’s an example instructions entry:

        I am a senior consultant working with mid-sized companies on operations and strategy projects. My typical tasks include client presentations, risk assessments, and stakeholder briefings. My audiences range from frontline managers to C-suite executives. I prefer bottom-line-up-front responses, concise structured output, and quantified trade-offs over qualitative generalities.

3.3.3. Start a fresh chat

  1. Resubmit the same baseline prompt.

  2. Reflect:

    1. Did Claude’s response shift from generic to context-aware?

    2. What did Claude assume about your meeting audience and topics?

    3. What additional profiles settings would tighten the response further?

3.4. Compare Built-in Styles

Claude ships with five preset styles: Normal, Concise, Formal, Learning, and Explanatory. They control tone, length, and structure — not content. The fastest way to feel the difference is to run the same prompt under each.

3.4.1. Start a new chat

  1. Pick a single business prompt to use as your control.

    • Here’s an example control prompt:

      Explain the difference between a project risk and a project issue, and provide one example of each in the context of a major program.

3.4.2. Submit the prompt under Normal (the default)

3.4.3. Switch the style and resubmit the prompt four more times

  1. Once each under Concise, Formal, Learning, and Explanatory.

  2. Switch styles via the "+" button in the lower-left corner of the chat input area, then Use style.

3.4.4. Compare the four responses side by side and reflect

Here are Anthropic’s descriptions of the built-in styles to guide your analysis:

  • Concise: Short and to the point, with minimal explanation or elaboration.

  • Formal: Professional and polished, with a tone suitable for business or academic communication.

  • Explanatory: Clear and detailed, with a focus on teaching and ensuring understanding.

  • Learning: Similar to Explanatory but with an even stronger emphasis on clarity, step-by-step explanations, and educational value.

Reflect:

  1. Which style produced the shortest response? Which produced the longest?

  2. Which style would you send to a busy executive? Which to a new team member who needs to learn the concept?

  3. Did any style misfit the prompt — too formal, too breezy, too pedagogical?

  4. Can you detect a difference between Explanatory and Learning, which are similar but not identical?

3.5. Create a Custom Style Using "Tailor to an Audience"

Custom styles let you go beyond the four presets. Claude offers three creation methods: uploading writing samples, describing the style in natural language, or starting from a template. The most natural starting point for stakeholder-driven communication is Tailor to an audience, which lets Claude generate the style instructions for you based on a description of who you’re writing to. Follow the steps below to build your custom style. You will choose one of two audience options:

  • Financial Brief (financial services and banking professionals)

  • Data Center Energy Brief (data center infrastructure professionals).

The steps are identical for both options; only the audience description and the test prompt differ.

3.5.1. Open the style menu

  1. In the chat interface, click "+" (lower left of the chat input area)

  2. Then select Use style

3.5.2. Open the style editor

  1. In the style dropdown, click Create & edit styles to open the Customize your styles modal

3.5.3. Start a new custom style

  1. Click Create custom style in the bottom left corner of the modal

  2. Choose the description method

  3. Click Describe style instead link to switch from the upload method to the describe method

5.1. Introduction

In this lab, you will move from understanding AI capabilities to making practical decisions about how to use AI in your own work. You will evaluate a realistic business scenario, surface the risks that should shape your delegation decisions, and determine where AI adds value and where human judgment remains essential. 5.2. Lab Overview

In this lab, you will complete the following activities:

  1. Identify the AI failure modes and risks that should shape AI delegation decisions in a hiring scenario

5.3. Scenario

You are a hiring manager at Northwind Logistics, a 1,200-employee U.S. third-party logistics company. You have an open role for a Senior Operations Analyst. This role is a mid-level individual contributor who will build dashboards, run cost analyses, and present findings to operations leadership. The salary range is $95K–$120K.

In the United States, hiring decisions are also governed by Title VII, ADA, and ADEA — federal laws that create real legal liability for discriminatory outcomes, including those produced by automated systems.

6.5. Revise the Prompt to Prevent the Failures

The original prompt was loose enough to invite the failures you just identified. Phrases like "any important context analysts are flagging" implicitly invited Claude to look beyond the document. Now you will rewrite the prompt to ground Claude strictly in the source.

6.5.1. Compose a revised prompt that

  1. Explicitly restricts Claude to the uploaded document

  2. Tells Claude what to do when information is not in the document (rather than guessing or inferring)

  3. Forbids external citations or references

  4. Specifies an output format that makes unsupported claims harder to slip in

    • Here is an example of a revised prompt to model on, or adapt directly:

      Using ONLY the content of the attached earnings release, draft a one-page briefing for portfolio managers. Apply these rules strictly:

      • Do not include any metric, statement, or data point not explicitly present in the document. If a common metric (e.g., EBITDA margin) is not disclosed, do not estimate or infer it.

      • Quote management commentary verbatim or not at all. Do not paraphrase quotes in ways that change meaning, and do not attribute statements to executives that are not in the document.

      • Do not cite or refer to external sources, analyst reports, or news coverage. The document is the only source.

      • For each claim in the briefing, the source must be traceable to a specific line in the document.

      • If you would normally include something but the document does not support it, write 'Not disclosed in source' instead.

6.7. Key Takeaways

Verification is non-negotiable for high-stakes AI output:

  • Hallucinations rarely look like obvious mistakes

  • Loose prompts invite confabulation; tight prompts with explicit grounding rules suppress it

  • A useful test of a grounded prompt is whether Claude refuses to answer questions the document cannot answer

  • For high-risk professional output, source verification before forwarding is mandatory not optional

6.8. Solution - Failure Modes Identified and Explained

Here are the three planted failures in the AI-generated brief. Each is a different failure mode category. All three are deliberately plausible — none looks obviously wrong on first reading. That is the point.

6.8.1. Failure 1: EBITDA margin of 28.5%

The claim in the brief:

"The company reported operating income of $24.1M and an EBITDA margin of 28.5%, marking a meaningful expansion from the prior year period."

Why it is wrong:

The source press release reports operating income of $24.1M, but it does not disclose EBITDA at all. The 28.5% figure is fabricated. Search the source for the term "EBITDA" — it does not appear.

Failure mode category: Fabricated metric

Why this one is the trickiest to catch:

EBITDA is such a routine metric in earnings reporting that a reader can easily skim past it. The 28.5% figure is also plausible — for a SaaS company at this revenue scale, a high-20s EBITDA margin is in the realistic range. Plausibility, however, is not truth.

6.8.2. Failure 2: $1B revenue target attributed to the CEO

The claim in the brief:

"In the earnings release, CEO Marcus Chen stated that the company expects to surpass $1 billion in annual revenue by the end of FY2027, citing continued strong execution against strategic priorities."

Why it is wrong:

Marcus Chen’s actual quote in the source is about three operational priorities: closing the top 50 enterprise pipeline opportunities, completing the integration of DataNexus, and continuing to invest in platform security. He says nothing about $1 billion in revenue, FY2027, or any specific revenue target.

Failure mode category: Misattributed or invented quote

Why this one is the highest-consequence failure:

Forward-looking financial statements attributed to a CEO can move a stock and create regulatory exposure. Putting words in an executive’s mouth — especially specific revenue targets and time frames — is one of the most consequential failure modes in financial AI output. A portfolio manager acting on this fabricated guidance could face real financial and reputational harm.

6.8.3. Failure 3: Bloomberg article on October 28

The claim in the brief:

"According to coverage in Bloomberg on October 28, sell-side analysts viewed the quarter as a meaningful upside surprise given concerns about enterprise IT spending in the prior quarter."

Why it is wrong:

There is no Bloomberg coverage cited or referenced anywhere in the source. The source is the press release alone — it contains no analyst commentary, no external articles, no third-party context. The Bloomberg citation, the October 28 date, and the analyst sentiment are all fabricated.

Failure mode category: Fabricated citation

Why this one is dangerous:

A fabricated citation gives the reader false confidence that an external source has been consulted. It also exposes the analyst to embarrassment if a portfolio manager tries to follow up on the cited article and cannot find it.
